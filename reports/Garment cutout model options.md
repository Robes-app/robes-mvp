# Clean garment cutouts need two models

No single tool on the market in November 2026 does the whole job, because "cut out this piece" is two jobs: choosing the right garment and tracing a clean edge. Background-removal tools (Photoroom, Bria, BiRefNet, Apple's subject lifting) trace good edges but keep whatever stands out in the picture, so a worn outfit or mirror selfie comes back as the whole person. Box-prompted segmenters (the SAM family) can take Robes' existing Gemini boxes and return one garment, but their masks are hard-edged and coarse. Cost will not decide this: the options run from free (on-device iOS) through about $0.0025–$0.005 per photo (hosted SAM 3) to about $0.02 per piece (Photoroom, Bria), and only remove.bg's successor at about $0.10 is out of line. Licence is the sharper filter: the planned D8 model, BiRefNet, is MIT-labelled but trained on a dataset whose terms forbid commercial use, and its author confirmed on 17 November 2026 that no permission exists and no clean version is coming. D8 therefore needs a deliberate decision, not a default: either accept that risk knowingly, or pay about $0.018 per piece for Bria's licensed model of the same design. No one has published a test of any of these on clothes, almost every price below comes from search summaries because vendor sites were blocked to the researchers, and nothing was run on a garment photo, so the bake-off on the team's own photos is the real evidence. The shortlist at the end names five candidates and what each must prove.

## Choosing the piece and tracing the edge are separate problems

The five hard cases in the brief split cleanly between the two jobs. Sheer fabric is an edge problem: a hard yes/no mask makes lace and mesh look "clipped, jagged, or unnaturally solid", and only a soft (alpha) mask can represent a pixel that is half fabric, half background ([CapCut](https://www.capcut.com/create/ai-background-removal-how-cutouts-work), [Deep-Image](https://deep-image.ai/blog/ai-background-removal-complex-edges-hair-glass/)). Hangers, mirror selfies and one garment off a worn outfit are choosing problems: a background remover labels each pixel foreground or background and has no idea of "this garment only" ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/salient-object-segmentation)). Black-on-black looks like an edge problem but behaves like a choosing problem: the BiRefNet-V2 paper reports that its worst failures are deciding what the foreground is, not tracing it, and that rival models fail on the same images, "a property of the paradigm" ([arXiv 2611.11272](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.11272)). Swapping one background remover for another will not fix it.

The table below is what the evidence predicts for each family. Every cell is an expectation from how the models work or from general (non-clothing) tests. **No source measured any of these on garments**, and the researchers found no independent clothing benchmark at all.

| Hard case | Background removers on the box crop (Photoroom, Bria, BiRefNet) | Box-prompted SAM family | Apple subject lifting (iOS 17+) | Gemini's own masks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sheer, lace, mesh | Best available, with a matting variant; Bria RMBG-2.0 and plain BiRefNet give near-binary masks | Poor: hard mask decoded at low resolution | Soft full-resolution mask; untested on fabric | Poor: a small stretched mask or a polygon |
| Black-on-black | Weak for all; a "wrong foreground" failure | Box tells it where to look; edge still coarse | Weak (contrast-driven) | Rough |
| Hanger | Keeps the hanger (it is attached to the subject) | Box alone probably keeps it; needs an extra point | Keeps it | Unknown |
| Mirror selfie | Returns the whole person and phone | Can return one garment | One instance: the whole person | Rough |
| One garment off a worn outfit | Cannot | The only family that can; layered pieces fuse | Cannot | Rough |

Three findings sharpen the picture. First, the box is a weaker prompt than it looks. One 2026 paper reports SAM 2 scoring about 0.63 with box-only prompts on a fashion dataset against about 0.80 when paired with a text-grounded detector, and a robotics paper reports SAM 2 fusing overlapping, similar-coloured garments into one mask with ragged boundaries; both figures come from search extracts and are indicative only ([GarmentSegAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.36506), [GarmentPile++](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.04158)). A box around a shirt under a jacket contains mostly jacket. Second, SAM's coarse edges are documented across the family: the HQ-SAM authors say SAM's masks fall short "particularly when dealing with objects that have intricate structures" ([HQ-SAM](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/SysCV/sam-hq/main/README.md)). Third, a garment cut from a worn outfit has holes where another piece covered it, and only an image generator can fill them, which is ruled out.

The design that follows is a pairing: a prompted model picks the piece and a background remover finishes the edge inside that region. Matte Anything already chains SAM to a matting model this way ([Matte Anything](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hustvl/Matte-Anything/main/README.md)), but nobody has measured the pairing on clothes. The likely end state is routing: a background remover for flat lays and hangers, a prompted model when the piece is worn, and today's rectangle when both fail.

Practice elsewhere supports excluding generators and buying rather than building. Vestiaire Collective tested several generative tools and dropped them because each altered item details, then chose Photoroom over its own in-house remover after an A/B test; Depop embedded Photoroom in 2025 ([Retail Tech Innovation Hub](https://retailtechinnovationhub.com/home/2026/10/13/vestiaire-collective-becomes-first-luxury-resale-platform-to-standardise-new-listing-photos-with-ai), [Depop](https://news.depop.com/depop-partners-with-photoroom-to-bring-ai-powered-photo-editing-to-millions/)). Try-on research also documents leftover clothing in a reference image "ghosting" into generated output ([NeurIPS 2025](https://neurips.cc/media/neurips-2025/Slides/116013.pdf)). That mechanism plausibly applies to Robes, where each piece's stored photo goes to the image generator as a reference (`platform/packages/jobs/src/handlers/look-render.ts:252-289`), so a clean cutout should help look quality as well as the wardrobe card. No source tested that on a general image model.

## Licences remove most of the free models, and reopen D8

The licence position differs by layer: code, trained weights, and the data the weights were trained on. The popular free cutout models are clean on the first two and not on the third.

| Model | Licence as labelled | Commercial use | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| SAM (2023), SAM 2 / 2.1, HQ-SAM, EdgeTAM, MobileSAM | Apache-2.0, code and weights | Yes | Read directly on GitHub (MobileSAM via search) |
| SAM 3 / 3.1 | Meta "SAM License": royalty-free use and distribution; bars military, nuclear, espionage uses; Meta may change terms; weights gated | Yes on its face | Licence text read directly |
| Bria RMBG-2.0 / 1.4 | CC BY-NC 4.0 | Only through Bria's paid API, an official listing, or a Bria licence (no public price) | Bria README read directly |
| BiRefNet (all variants) | MIT, code and weights | Author says yes; training data says no | Repo and issues read directly; dataset terms via search |
| BEN2 | MIT | Same training-data problem | Repo read directly; weights label via search |
| IS-Net, `@imgly/background-removal` | Apache-2.0 code / AGPL-3.0 package | Unanswered / copyleft | Read directly |
| ZIM (box in, soft mask out) | CC BY-NC 4.0 | No | Read directly |
| FastSAM | AGPL-3.0 | Copyleft | Read directly |
| SegFormer clothes models, Sapiens v1 | NVIDIA non-commercial / CC BY-NC | No | NVIDIA licence read directly; model cards via search |
| `rembg` library | MIT, but its default model is now Bria's | Not with defaults | Read directly |

**BiRefNet is the case that matters, because D8 is built on it.** The repository is MIT and the author wrote on 24 October 2026 that "you can use all the codes / model weights under the MIT license" ([BiRefNet issue #306](https://github.com/ZhengPeng7/BiRefNet/issues/306)). But the general model was trained on DIS5K, whose terms of use allow non-commercial research and education only and prohibit commercial use "even after processing" without the original authors' permission (via search: [DIS5K terms](https://huggingface.co/datasets/not-lain/DIS5K/resolve/main/DIS5K%20Dataset%20Terms%20of%20Use.pdf?download=true)). Asked on 16 November 2026 whether that permission was obtained or a commercially clean version exists, the author answered the next day: "No, and it's almost impossible to do that" ([BiRefNet issue #331](https://github.com/ZhengPeng7/BiRefNet/issues/331)). The dataset's authors have left a request for written permission unanswered since August ([DIS issue #150](https://github.com/xuebinqin/DIS/issues/150)). Whether a dataset restriction binds a model trained on it is legally unsettled, and Cloudflare ships BiRefNet in a commercial product ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/background-removal/)), so the risk is unquantified, not proven. None of this is legal advice.

### What this does to the D8 plan

The repo's plan is fal-hosted BiRefNet with Photoroom as the quality fallback, stored once per piece as WebP with alpha (`platform/docs/infrastructure.md:120`, `platform/docs/reference/cost-research.md`). Three things have changed since it was written on 24 September 2026.

The licence question is now open and on the record. Calling BiRefNet through fal does not obviously move it: the notes found nothing saying a host absorbs a training-data problem. The licensed alternative is the same design, since Bria RMBG-2.0 is built on the BiRefNet architecture and trained on more than 15,000 licensed, hand-labelled images ([Bria README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Bria-AI/RMBG-2.0/dev/README.md)), and it is on fal at $0.018 per image (via search: [fal](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/bria/background/remove)). At 10,000 pieces a month that is about $180 against roughly $10–$25 for BiRefNet on fal. The price of removing the question is therefore about $160 a month at that volume.

D8 also predates the multi-piece read. It assumed one piece per photo; testers now photograph themselves wearing an outfit, and PR #242 says so ("her piece photos are mostly her wearing them with a whole outfit"). BiRefNet and Photoroom both fail that case in the same way, so the fallback does not cover the primary's weakness. D8 as written delivers flat lays and hangers only.

Finally, fal's BiRefNet price is unconfirmed. fal's own page shows "$0 per compute second" while a fal article says $0.0008 per second ([fal BiRefNet v2](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/birefnet/v2/llms.txt), [fal article](https://fal.ai/learn/tools/best-background-remover-apis-2026)); the cost doc's "$0.001–0.002" is a derived figure. D8 stays in the bake-off as one candidate among five, paired against Bria so the licence premium buys a measured quality answer.

## Cost runs from free to ten cents; speed is mostly unmeasured

Prices below are per piece unless marked. Only the repo's own figures and the GitHub-published timings were read on a primary page; **every vendor price is a search-summary reading from 21 November 2026 and must be confirmed on the live page before budgeting.**

| Option | Cost | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Apple subject lifting; iOS 27 prompted segmentation | $0 | OS frameworks with no fee or quota in the API (researcher's reading of Apple's pages) |
| Self-hosted BiRefNet on a serverless GPU | about $0.0002–$0.001 | Researcher's arithmetic from third-party GPU rates; T4/L4 timings unsourced |
| BiRefNet on fal | about $0.001–$0.0025 | Derived from a disputed per-second rate |
| Hosted SAM 3 | $0.0025 per image (Meta), $0.005 per request (fal) | Search extracts ([Meta](https://dev.meta.ai/models/sam-3-1), [fal](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/sam-3/image)) |
| Bria RMBG-2.0 (fal or Bria) | $0.018 | Search summary |
| Photoroom Remove Background | $0.02; $0.01 on its partner plan near 100k images a month | Search summary ([Photoroom](https://www.photoroom.com/api/pricing), [partner plan](https://www.photoroom.com/api/api-partner-plan)) |
| Cloudinary `e_background_removal` | 75 transformations per asset; about $0.03 in the repo's cost doc | Count via search ([Cloudinary](https://cloudinary.com/documentation/transformation_counts)); dollar value unverified |
| Pixelcut | $0.05 | Search summary ([Pixelcut](https://pixelcut.ai/docs/developer-guide/getting-started/pricing)) |
| Leonardo.Ai (remove.bg's successor) | $0.1047 | Search summary ([Leonardo docs](https://docs.leonardo.ai/docs/background-removal)) |

Two points change how these compare. **SAM is priced per photo, the cutout APIs per piece.** A mirrored copy of fal's schema says several boxes go in one request, so a photo with five pieces would cost $0.005 through SAM 3 and $0.09–$0.10 through Bria or Photoroom ([schema mirror](https://www.sandbase.ai/model/meta/sam-3/image/docs)). That per-request billing with many boxes is an inference and needs one live call to confirm. And for scale, one generated look image costs Robes $0.067 (`platform/packages/ai/src/models.ts:58-65`), so even the dearest sensible cutout is a third of a single render, paid once per piece.

Speed evidence is thin. BiRefNet's author measures 57–95 ms per 1024-pixel image on an A100 or RTX 4090 ([BiRefNet](https://github.com/ZhengPeng7/BiRefNet)); Photoroom claims about 450 ms in its own benchmark ([Photoroom](https://www.photoroom.com/inside-photoroom/background-remover-benchmark)); Meta quotes 30 ms per image for SAM 3 on an H200 ([Ultralytics SAM 3 docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/main/docs/en/models/sam-3.md)). No source gives a cold-start time for fal or Replicate, a hosted latency for a box-prompted SAM call, or any Apple figure. Running BiRefNet on the existing CPU containers is out: the lite model alone needs 2.4–6.5 s and up to 10.5 GB of memory at full resolution on a fast laptop chip (via search: [senty-au](https://huggingface.co/senty-au/BiRefNet_lite-ONNX-dynamic)). All the server options should fit D8's target of 12 seconds at the 95th percentile, but that is to be measured in the bake-off, cold starts included.

### Three options that look attractive and are not

Gemini's own masks would add no vendor, but they are a rough, text-encoded mask that arrives malformed often enough for tutorials to recommend validating every response, and their future is poor. Google's Gemini 3 guide says pixel masks are not supported on Gemini 3 Pro or Flash, current docs describe a polygon outline instead, and the one model Google recommended for masks is the one reported to be shutting down (all via search: [Gemini 3 guide](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/gemini-3), [image understanding docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/image-understanding)). Google's cookbook, read directly, points the other way for later 3.x models, so the sources conflict ([cookbook](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google-gemini/cookbook/main/quickstarts/Spatial_understanding.ipynb)). The boxes are the reusable asset, not the masks.

Cloudinary would add no vendor for the legacy app, but the platform has already moved to S3 and CloudFront, the legacy database is frozen, and on the numbers available it costs more than Photoroom with no published quality evidence on clothes.

Leonardo.Ai is remove.bg under a new name at five times Photoroom's price.

### Recording the cost from day one

The platform ledger already fits a non-Gemini call. `ledger.record` takes a feature, model, family, image count, latency, status and `costMicros` (`platform/packages/db/src/repos/ledger.ts:5-75`), so a cutout is a row with a new feature such as `wardrobe.cutout`, `images: 1` and zero tokens. A new model family needs its own line in `DAILY_BUDGET_MICROS`, or it falls under the $10-a-day default (`platform/packages/core/src/budget/index.ts:1-14`). Per-second billing (fal BiRefNet) means the cost per piece varies and should be computed from measured time, not a constant. An on-device cutout costs nothing, and recording it as a zero-cost row is a design choice that would show how many pieces each route handles. The legacy log has no cost column and cannot change, so this belongs on the platform.

Storage needs one decision before building. `master.jpg` is flattened onto white as a JPEG and cannot hold transparency (`platform/packages/media/src/render.ts:45-60`), and the repo carries two unwired scaffolds for a cutout: a second asset through `wardrobe_items.cutout_asset_id`, and a `cutout.webp` beside the renditions flagged by `assets.has_cutout`. The brief has to pick one.

## iOS lifts the easy cases free; worn outfits wait for iOS 27

Apple gives two free on-device routes, and they map onto the two jobs. Subject lifting (`VNGenerateForegroundInstanceMaskRequest`, iOS 17+) returns a **soft mask at the full input resolution** for each thing that stands out, with no prompt ([WWDC23 session 10176](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10176/)). It can use Robes' box after the fact: read which instance sits under the box and keep that one. But Apple says outright that "subjects are not always single objects", so a dressed person is one instance and touching pieces on a bed will probably merge. It suits a single piece on a contrasting surface, which is also the case where it is hardest to beat on edge quality for nothing.

The second route arrived with iOS 27 in September 2026: `GenerateIterativeSegmentationRequest` segments the object at a seed point, with refinement points, and Apple's own session names "a piece of clothing" as an example ([WWDC26 session 237](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/237/)). Seeded with the centre of a Gemini box, it is the first free route that could take one garment off a worn outfit. The session demonstrates a box input but shows code only for points; the model downloads on first use; and its speed, mask resolution and oldest supported iPhone are not documented in anything the researchers could read. Core AI on iOS 27 can also run SAM 3 with text prompts, as a 623 MB on-demand download, which is too heavy as a default ([WWDC26 session 326](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/326/)).

Reaching either from the Expo app means a development build and, realistically, a small Swift module of the team's own: the existing open-source wrappers return one whole-foreground cutout and expose no per-instance masks, and none wraps the iOS 27 request ([expo-segmentation](https://github.com/westwood-dev/expo-segmentation)). Subject lifting does not run in the iOS Simulator, so tests need a device ([Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/764948)). Nothing found restricts storing or commercially using the output; one clause, quoted second-hand, bars using Apple-model output to train another model ([Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/836435)).

On-device does not remove the need for a server route. The boxes come back from the server read, so the phone cuts after the read returns and uploads the result. The web has no built-in equivalent, and its options are a 130–200 MB model download or a copyleft library. Phones below iOS 27 cannot isolate a worn garment. Since web may lag, the simplest web path is not to cut out in the browser: upload the photo and let the server do it. iOS on-device is then a free first attempt in front of a paid server route that both clients share.

## A five-candidate bake-off, and what each must prove

Run every candidate on the same set of the team's own photos, with today's rectangle crop as the control. The notes suggest about 80–100 photos spread across the hard cases plus 20–30 easy ones; that size is the researchers' judgement, as no source gives one. Each cutout gets a verdict of usable, usable with fallback, or unusable, and any missing part of the garment fails it outright. Judge on the white card and again on mid-grey, because white hides pale halos. Report per hard case, not as one average. Record cost and wall-clock time per piece for every call, cold starts included, which also exercises the ledger.

| Candidate | Role | Expected cost | Licence | What it must prove |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Apple subject lifting, picked by the Gemini box (iOS 17+, real device) | Free first attempt on flat lays and hangers | $0 | No restriction found | That the instance under the box matches the box on most single-piece photos; that a mismatch is detectable so it can hand off; what it does with hangers and touching pieces; time on a mid-range iPhone |
| 2. iOS 27 prompted segmentation, seeded by the box centre | Free route for worn pieces | $0 | Same | That it returns one garment from a worn outfit and a mirror selfie; whether a box input exists in the shipped API; download size, speed and device floor; whether edges are good enough to store |
| 3. Hosted box-prompted SAM (SAM 3 on fal or Meta's API; SAM 2.1 as the Apache-2.0 reference), all boxes in one call | Server route for worn pieces and web | $0.0025–$0.005 per photo | Apache-2.0 (2.1) or SAM License (3) | That one call with every box is billed once; that the box runs in single-object mode, not "find all similar"; how often layered pieces fuse; whether the hard edge is acceptable on a card, or needs candidate 4 or 5 to finish it |
| 4. Bria RMBG-2.0 on the box crop | Licensed edge model for flat lays and hangers | $0.018 | Licensed through the API | That it justifies roughly ten times BiRefNet's price; black-on-black, where one author-run benchmark puts it weakest; what its near-binary mask does to sheer fabric. Confirm first that fal's RMBG-3.0 is not a generative model before testing it |
| 5. BiRefNet on fal, general and matting variants, on the box crop (the D8 plan) | Cheap edge model; the only shortlisted one with a soft mask for sheer | about $0.001–$0.0025, to be measured | MIT label, DIS5K question open | That its quality matches Bria and Photoroom; its real cost per piece; and separately from the test, a yes or no from whoever owns legal risk |

Photoroom at $0.02 belongs in the run as the commercial yardstick the resale platforms chose, not as a sixth contender: if neither 4 nor 5 matches it on flat lays, it becomes the edge model. Before sending real photos, settle its terms in writing, because its pages disagree on whether self-serve API images train its models ([security page](https://www.photoroom.com/platform/security), [privacy policy](https://www.photoroom.com/legal/privacy-policy)). Any server candidate is a new processor of her photos and goes in `platform/docs/data-inventory.md`.

Two test variables cost little and matter. The crop Robes makes today pads the box by 8% of the whole frame with a 28% minimum side (`platform/packages/core/src/photo-pieces/index.ts:33-52`), which pulls neighbours into a small item's crop, so candidates 4 and 5 should be run on a tight box crop as well. And the pairing of 3 with 4 or 5 (SAM's mask limiting where the edge model may keep pixels) should be tried on the worn photos, since it is the only route to both the right piece and a soft edge.

## Conclusion

The cutout decision is less a choice of vendor than a choice of route per photo type, and the bake-off's most useful output is the share of the team's real photos that fall in each type. If most pieces arrive worn, the edge model matters little and the prompted segmenter carries the feature; if most are flat lays, free on-device lifting plus one licensed edge model covers nearly everything for close to nothing. That split also sets how much the BiRefNet licence question is worth arguing over: at wardrobe volumes the clean alternative costs tens to low hundreds of dollars a month, which is small against an open commercial-use question on every stored image.

A cutout can also fail in a way a rectangle cannot, by dropping a sleeve, so the stored record should keep the original crop beside it and the feature should fall back to the crop when the result looks wrong. The checks proposed for that (mask size against the box, stray islands, a yes/no vision check) are unpublished for clothing and need calibrating on the same test set.
